How our recommender system works

What decides whose profiles reach you. This is not a legal document — it is an honest description of a fairly simple piece of software.

Last updated 5 November 2026. Describes the app as it works today.

The whole thing, in one sentence. We take everybody you have not already swiped on, remove anyone outside the age range and gender you asked for, and shuffle what is left. There is no scoring, no ranking and no desirability model. Ten profiles at a time, in random order.

Step 1: who is taken out

Before anything else, three groups never appear in your deck.

  • You.
  • Anybody you have already swiped on, whatever you chose — like, pass, super like, "not interested" or report. A profile you have seen does not come back, so passing on somebody is permanent from your side.
  • Accounts we have removed.

People who have liked you are not taken out. They stay in the ordinary deck and you are not told they are interested, so a match can happen from any swipe. The one exception is a super like: that one is shown to you, with a banner on the card, because telling you is the point of it.

Step 2: the two filters

Two of your preferences are applied as hard filters. Somebody outside them is not shown to you at all, ever.

Age
The range you set. We work it out from dates of birth rather than stored ages, so it stays right on everybody's birthday.
Gender
Who you said you wanted to see.

A profile that has not filled in its age or gender yet is kept rather than hidden, so people still setting up are not invisible.

What your other preferences do today: nothing

You can also tell the app your preferred height range, and your preferences about smoking, drinking, intentions, children and languages. We store them, you can see and change them, and the deck does not yet use them. They are there for the ranking work described at the bottom of this page. We would rather say that than let you believe a filter is working when it is not.

One filter you should know is one-directional

Your preferences decide who you see. They do not yet decide who sees you. Somebody whose age range or gender preference you fall outside can still be shown your profile and can still like you. If they do and you like them back, you will match normally.

This is a known limitation and we are fixing it. Until we do, a like you send is not a guarantee that the other person's filters would ever have shown them to you.

Step 3: the order

Random. Genuinely random — the server shuffles the remaining profiles and hands back ten.

That means none of the following affects your position in anybody's deck, or theirs in yours:

  • How attractive anyone is, by any measure. There is no score attached to any account.
  • How many likes you have had, given, or received.
  • How often you open the app, or how recently.
  • How long you have been a member.
  • Whether anybody has super liked you — that shows a banner, and nothing more.
  • Anything you could pay for. There is nothing to pay for.
  • Your religion, politics, sexual orientation or pronouns. Nothing in the app filters, ranks or sorts on any of these. They appear on your profile because you chose to put them there, and that is their only effect.
  • Your university, your course, or your city. Your city is text on your profile and nothing reads it.
  • Your location. We do not have it — see the Privacy Statement.

What this means for you

Mostly that there is not much to game. Two things are genuinely in your hands:

  • Your filters decide the size of your pool. A narrow age range on a service with a few thousand students is the single biggest reason a deck runs dry. Widening it takes effect immediately — the deck refills without restarting the app.
  • Your profile decides what people do when they reach it. Since the order is random, a photo and a bio are the entire difference between a like and a pass.

And passing is permanent, so it is worth opening a profile you are unsure about rather than flicking it away.

What we plan to change

We are working on ordering the deck rather than shuffling it. The signals we intend to use, in rough order of weight:

  1. Whether somebody is actually around. An active account before a dormant one — a like that nobody will ever see is worse than no like.
  2. Whether they super liked you. Shown already, and it should also come first.
  3. New profiles, briefly, so somebody who joined today is not buried under everybody who joined before them.
  4. How well your stated preferences overlap — the smoking, drinking, intentions, children and language preferences above, used as a nudge in the ordering rather than as a wall. We would rather show you somebody who is a near-fit than show you nobody.
  5. Same university or department, as a mild preference. Shared ground is the thing this app has that a general dating app does not.

There will still be no attractiveness score, nothing sold, and no use of special category data. When this ships we will update this page before it goes live, not after.

Why we publish this. Article 27 of the EU Digital Services Act requires online platforms to set out the main parameters of their recommender systems in plain language. As a micro enterprise we are exempt from it under Article 19. We publish it anyway, because the exemption runs out the moment we grow and because "how does this decide what I see" is a fair question whatever the law says.

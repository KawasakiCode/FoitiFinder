How our recommender system works

What decides whose profiles reach you. This is not a legal document — it is an honest description of a fairly simple piece of software.

Last updated 8 November 2026. Describes the app as it works today.

The whole thing, in one sentence. We take everybody you have not already swiped on, remove anyone who falls outside your filters — or whose own filters you fall outside of — give each of the rest a small score, and then draw twenty of them at random, where a higher score makes a card more likely to come out early but never guarantees it. There is no attractiveness score and nothing is for sale.

Step 1: who is taken out

Before anything else, these never appear in your deck.

  • You.
  • Anybody you have already swiped on, whatever you chose — like, pass, super like, "not interested" or report. A profile you have seen does not come back, so passing on somebody is permanent from your side.
  • Anybody with no photos, and anybody who has not finished setting up their profile. A card with no face on it is a grey rectangle with a name, and offering you one spends a swipe on nothing.
  • Accounts we have removed.

People who have liked you are not taken out. They stay in the ordinary deck and you are not told they are interested, so a match can happen from any swipe. The one exception is a super like: that one is shown to you, with a banner on the card, because telling you is the point of it.

Step 2: the filters, in both directions

Four of your preferences are applied as hard filters. Somebody outside them is not shown to you at all, ever.

Age
The range you set. We work it out from dates of birth rather than stored ages, so it stays right on everybody's birthday.
Gender
Who you said you wanted to see.
Height
The range you set, against the height on their profile.
Languages
At least one language in common, and that is all — a second or third shared language earns nothing. Two people either understand each other or they do not.

And all four are applied the other way round. You are only shown somebody whose own age range includes you, whose own gender preference accepts you, and whose own height range includes you. So a card in your deck is always somebody who could have had you in theirs.

This is the point of it: a like you send can be returned. One-directional filtering means the deck offers people who are never going to be shown your profile, and a right swipe on one of them cannot become a match whatever they do. It costs a smaller deck, and that is the right trade — a like that cannot be returned is worse than a card you never saw.

A profile that has not filled in an age range, a gender preference, a height or a language yet is kept, in every one of these checks, in both directions. Not having answered is not the same as saying no, and hiding those people would make the app useless for anybody who just joined.

What your other preferences do today: nothing

You can also state preferences about smoking, drinking, intentions, children, and whether to be shown people outside your city. We store them, you can see and change them, and the deck does not use them. We would rather say that than let you believe a filter is working when it is not.

Step 3: the score

Everybody who survives step 2 is given a score. It is the sum of four things and nothing else.

SignalWorth
They super liked you 3 points. The strongest thing anybody can say in this app, and the only interest the deck discloses — somebody spending one of their two a week on you is a deliberate act, where being online is an accident of timing.
How recently they were last active Up to 2 points, fading smoothly rather than at a cutoff: about 2 for somebody using the app now, 0.7 after three days, 0.1 after nine. A like that nobody will ever open is worse than no like.
Each interest you have in common 1 point each. The catalogue lets everybody pick up to ten interests, so this runs from 0 to 10 — which means it can outweigh everything else added together. That is deliberate: shared ground is what this app is for.
Same university 1 point. Shared ground again, and the thing this app has that a general dating app does not.

Nothing else contributes. In particular, there is no score attached to an account that follows it around: the number above is worked out fresh for each viewer every time a deck is filled, and the same person scores differently for different people. Nobody is ranked globally.

Step 4: the order

The deck is not sorted by that score. It is a weighted random draw — a higher score raises the chance of coming out early and never guarantees it, and a score of zero still gets a ticket.

This matters, and it is worth being plain about why. Sorting by score would hand you the same faces every time the deck refilled, and the rest of the pool would never be seen however well it scored. The people at the top would collect all of the attention, which is exactly the feedback loop this design avoids everywhere else. Drawing rather than sorting also keeps the deck feeling different each time.

You get twenty cards at a time, and the deck asks for more before it runs out.

What has no bearing on it at all

  • How attractive anyone is, by any measure. There is no desirability model, no ELO, no beauty score, and nothing that learns one from the way people swipe.
  • How many likes you have had, given, or received.
  • How long you have been a member.
  • Anything you could pay for. There is nothing to pay for.
  • Your religion, politics or sexual orientation. These are special category data under Article 9 GDPR, and the code that builds the deck does not read them — see section 3 of the Privacy Statement. They appear on your profile because you chose to put them there, and that is their only effect.
  • Your personality tags and your MBTI type. We collect them and we show them, and we deliberately do not score them. Similar personalities do not predict compatibility and neither do opposite ones, so weighting them would mean inventing a rule and imposing it on everybody. They are on your card to give somebody something to react to.
  • Your pronouns.
  • Your course, your job, or your city. Your city is text on your profile and nothing reads it.
  • Your location. We do not have it — see the Privacy Statement.

What this means for you

Three things are genuinely in your hands:

  • Your filters decide the size of your pool. A narrow age or height range on a service with a few thousand students is the single biggest reason a deck runs dry — and because the filters run both ways, a narrow range also takes you out of other people's decks. Widening takes effect immediately; the deck refills without restarting the app.
  • Your interests decide where you tend to land. It is the heaviest signal in the score, and the only one you can move by filling something in.
  • Opening the app matters a little. Being recently active makes your card more likely to come out early. We would rather tell you that than pretend it is not there.

And passing is permanent, so it is worth opening a profile you are unsure about rather than flicking it away.

What we might change

Two things we have considered and not built: a brief boost for profiles that joined today, so somebody new is not drawn behind everybody who joined before them; and some weight on whether a person tends to answer the matches they make, since a match nobody confirms helps nobody. Neither is in the app today.

There will still be no attractiveness score, nothing sold, and no use of special category data. When any of this changes we will update this page before it goes live, not after.

Why we publish this. Article 27 of the EU Digital Services Act requires online platforms to set out the main parameters of their recommender systems in plain language. As a micro enterprise we are exempt from it under Article 19. We publish it anyway, because the exemption runs out the moment we grow and because "how does this decide what I see" is a fair question whatever the law says.
